Open a Business Bank Account Without an EIN

Starting a small business as a sole proprietor often raises the question: can you open a business bank account without an EIN? The short answer is yes. Many banks let sole proprietors open a business bank account using their Social Security Number (SSN) instead of an Employer Identification Number (EIN). This guide walks you through why you might choose to do this, step-by-step instructions, which banks commonly allow it, documents needed, options while your EIN is pending, and common mistakes to avoid.

Why open a separate business bank account as a sole proprietor?

  • Keep personal and business finances separate: Separating accounts simplifies bookkeeping and tax preparation, and makes it easier to track business income and expenses.
  • Build credibility with customers and vendors: Accepting checks or payments in a business account looks more professional.
  • Protect personal liability and financial organization: While a sole proprietor doesn’t have the liability protection of an LLC, separation reduces the risk of commingling funds and clarifies business performance.
  • Access to business tools: Business accounts often include merchant services, payroll options, and business debit/credit cards.

Can you open a business bank account without an EIN?

Yes. Most banks will allow a sole proprietor to open a business checking or savings account using an SSN in place of an EIN. The bank typically treats the account as a “sole proprietorship” account and uses your name and SSN for tax reporting (1099s, interest statements, etc.).

However, policies vary by bank and sometimes by branch or account type. Larger national banks commonly accept SSNs for sole proprietors, while some business-only accounts or accounts with specific features may require an EIN.

Step-by-step: How to open a business bank account without an EIN

1. Decide what type of account you need

Choose between a basic business checking account, a business savings account, or a combined package. Think about monthly transaction limits, fees, minimum balances, online banking, and merchant services.

2. Gather required documents

Most banks will require a combination of the following when you open a sole proprietor business bank account with an SSN:

  • Valid government-issued photo ID (driver’s license, passport, state ID)
  • Social Security Number (SSN) or Social Security card — many banks accept the SSN printed on your ID, but some may ask to see the Social Security card
  • Proof of business name
    • If you operate under your legal name (e.g., Jane Doe doing business as Jane Doe), a separate DBA may not be required.
    • If you use a trade name, bring a "Doing Business As" (DBA) or fictitious name filing from your county or state.
  • Business license or permit, if your city or state requires one for your type of business
  • Business address (a home address is acceptable for sole proprietors)
  • Estimated annual revenue and anticipated monthly transactions (banks often ask these for account setup)

3. Compare banks and account terms

Shop for features that matter to you: monthly fees, fee waivers, free transactions, ATM access, mobile deposit, integrations with accounting software, and customer service. If you plan to accept card payments, check merchant service options and fees.

4. Apply in person or online

Many banks allow online account opening, but for sole proprietors using an SSN and a DBA you may prefer visiting a branch. In-person visits can be smoother if the bank requests any local filings or if staff need to verify DBA paperwork.

5. Fund the account and set up services

Bring enough to meet the minimum opening deposit. Once open, order checks, request a business debit card, enroll in online banking, and set up any merchant services or bill pay needed.

Banks that commonly allow business accounts without an EIN

Policies change, but the following bank types and specific banks often allow sole proprietors to open business accounts using an SSN instead of an EIN:

  • Large national banks: Bank of America, Chase, Wells Fargo, and Citibank typically permit sole proprietors to open business checking with SSN. Requirements vary by account tier and branch.
  • Regional banks and credit unions: Many regional banks and credit unions accept SSNs for sole proprietors and can be a good option for more personalized service.
  • Online banks and fintechs: Online business banks and fintech platforms (such as BlueVine, Novo, Mercury for certain solo founders) often allow account opening with SSN, though some fintechs require an EIN for certain business entity types.

Tip: Check each bank’s account disclosures or call customer service to confirm policy changes and documentation requirements before visiting or applying.

Open a business account with SSN instead of EIN: what to expect

  • Tax reporting: Banks will report interest and other taxable items to the IRS using your SSN.
  • Privacy considerations: Using an SSN ties the account directly to your personal taxpayer identity. If you later form an LLC or obtain an EIN, you can request to update the account to the new business EIN.
  • Credit and lending: Business credit history may be limited initially. Some lenders consider personal credit history for sole proprietors who use SSNs.

EIN pending? Options for opening an account

If you applied for an EIN but haven’t received it yet, some banks will allow you to open the account and update the EIN later. Options include:

  • Open with SSN, update later: Start the account with your SSN and bring the EIN paperwork when it’s issued to update the account records.
  • Bring IRS application receipt: If you applied online or by fax, bring documentation or an EIN application confirmation; some banks will accept proof that your EIN is pending.
  • Wait for EIN issuance: If you prefer, you can wait; obtaining an EIN online from the IRS is typically immediate if you apply on the IRS website.

Note: If banks require an EIN for certain services (like payroll accounts or multiple-signature accounts), ask if you can open a basic account first and upgrade once the EIN is issued.

Documents needed to open a business bank account as a sole proprietor (quick checklist)

  • Primary photo ID (driver’s license, passport)
  • Social Security Number (SSN) or card
  • DBA/Fictitious name filing (if using a trade name)
  • Business license or permit (if applicable)
  • Business address and contact details
  • Initial deposit (check or cash)

Common mistakes and misconceptions to avoid

  • Assuming all banks treat sole proprietors the same: Policies vary—call ahead or read disclosures to confirm SSN acceptance and documents required.
  • Using personal accounts for business: Mixing finances complicates taxes and can hurt professional image. Open a separate business account even as a sole proprietor.
  • Not getting a DBA when needed: If you operate under a trade name different from your legal name, failing to register a DBA can lead to compliance or acceptance issues at the bank.
  • Delaying EIN if you plan to hire or add employees: You’ll need an EIN to hire employees and for certain tax filings—get one before you recruit staff.
  • Missing account fees and limits: Carefully check monthly fees, transaction limits, and fee waivers to avoid unexpected charges.

Practical tips and examples

  • Example workflow: Sarah runs a freelance graphic design business as a sole proprietor. She registers a DBA with her county, compares two local banks, visits a branch with her ID, SSN, DBA certificate, and $100 opening deposit, and opens a business checking account that integrates with her invoicing software.
  • Consider a business debit card: Use a business debit card for business purchases to keep records tidy. Request a card when opening the account.
  • Ask about online accounting integrations: Choose a bank that integrates with QuickBooks, Xero, or your invoicing app to save bookkeeping time.
  • If you run a home-based business: Your home address is typically acceptable; if you worry about privacy, consider a registered agent service for an LLC later or a P.O. Box in addition to the physical address requirement (banks often need a physical address for identity verification).

When to get an EIN anyway

Even though you can open a sole proprietor business bank account without an EIN, applying for one is often free and simple via the IRS website. Consider getting an EIN if you:

  • Plan to hire employees
  • Want to separate your SSN from business transactions for privacy
  • Are forming an LLC, partnership, or corporation
  • Need account features that some banks restrict to entities with EINs

Conclusion

Opening a sole proprietor business bank account without an EIN is a common and achievable step for new business owners. Most banks accept an SSN in place of an EIN for sole proprietorships, provided you bring primary ID, proof of your business name (DBA if applicable), and any local licenses. Compare banks for fee structure, features, and integrations. If you anticipate hiring or want further separation between your personal and business tax identity, obtain an EIN—either before or shortly after opening your account. Separating your finances early makes taxes, bookkeeping, and growth easier, so take the step now even if you don’t yet have an EIN.

Frequently asked questions

Can I open a business bank account without an EIN?

Yes, if you're a sole proprietor. Many banks let you open a business account with your SSN, a photo ID, and your business name. LLCs and employers need an EIN.

Do I need an EIN as a sole proprietor?

Not to open an account, but an EIN is free from the IRS, protects your SSN privacy, and is required if you hire employees or form an LLC.

What documents do I need to open a business bank account?

Generally a government photo ID, your SSN or EIN, and your business name or registration. Requirements vary by bank and business structure.

How long does it take to get an EIN?

Applying online through the IRS takes only a few minutes, and you receive the EIN immediately at the end of the session — at no cost.

Is it worth separating business and personal banking?

Yes. It simplifies taxes, makes bookkeeping cleaner, and supports the liability protection of an LLC if you form one later.
