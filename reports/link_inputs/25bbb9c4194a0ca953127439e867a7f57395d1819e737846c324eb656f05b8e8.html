Pricing freelance work is hard because the number you choose affects both your income and the clients you attract. To price freelance work without undercharging, set a floor rate from your costs and time, price by scope and value rather than hours alone, and write payment terms before you start — so the price, the work, and the payment terms are agreed before the first task begins. If you are still looking for those first projects, see How to Find Your First Freelance Clients 2026.

Key takeaways:

  • Set a floor rate from your costs, tax, and the time the work actually takes.
  • Price by scope and value, not hours alone.
  • Compare pricing approaches before you choose one for a project.
  • Write payment terms before you start, including milestones and late payment.
  • Review your prices after a few projects, and raise them when your work or demand changes.

Start with a floor rate, not a guess

Start with what the work must cover: your time, your tools, and your taxes. Add a margin for time you spend on work that is not billed to a client, such as finding work, answering messages, and preparing invoices. That total is your floor rate — the lowest figure you can accept without losing money on the work. Check the invoicing tools you use, and see how receipt attachment works in an invoice.

Price by scope and value, not hours alone

An hourly rate can undervalue work that takes little time but needs rare skill. Before you quote, ask what the work is worth to the client: a single page of copy, a fixed number of revisions, or a result tied to a deadline. Price the scope, the number of revisions, and the value the client gets, and say what is included in the price so extra work is charged separately.

Write payment terms before you start

Agree the terms before you start, not after the work is done. Set out when payment is due, how much is paid at the start, and when the final payment is due. For larger work, ask for payment in milestones — for example, a deposit, a payment at the mid-point, and a final payment on delivery. That way the client knows what to expect and you are paid at each stage. Crunch’s guide to essential freelancer contracts describes payment milestones, a deposit or retainer, and a late payment clause, which gives the terms a practical shape.

Review your prices, do not set them once

Your first price for a service is a starting point, not a fixed number. After a few projects, look at how long the work took, whether the price covered your costs, and whether clients accepted the price quickly. If the work took longer than planned, or if clients accepted at once and asked for more work, your price may be too low. Raise it in small steps for new clients, and let regular clients know in advance if a price changes.

FAQ

How do I calculate my freelance rate?

Start with your floor rate — your costs, tax, and time — then check the scope and value of the work. That figure is the lowest you can accept; the price you quote for a project should sit at or above it.

Should I charge per hour or per project?

Charge per hour when the work is unclear or likely to change, and per project when the scope and deadline are fixed. For work where the result matters most, price by the value the client gets rather than the hours you spend.

What if a client says my price is too high?

Ask what they need and what their budget is, then change the scope — for example, fewer revisions or less work — rather than cutting the rate for the same work. If the budget cannot cover your floor rate, it is better to let the work go than to accept it at a loss.

How do I ask for payment in advance?

Put the deposit in your written terms before you start, with how much is due and when the final payment is due. The client should know the payment plan before the first task begins.

When should I raise my freelance prices?

Raise your prices after you have finished a few projects and can see how long the work took and what it cost. Tell regular clients before the new price applies, and apply higher prices to new clients first.
