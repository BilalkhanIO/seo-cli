Finding your first freelance clients is mostly about being easy to hire, not about being everywhere at once. To find your first freelance clients, start with people who already know your work, show one clear example of what you can do, ask for a short first project with a clear scope and price, and follow up once in writing — so a client can say yes to a small job before they trust you with a big one. This is general information, not legal or financial advice, and local contract and tax rules differ.

Key takeaways:

  • Start with people who already know your work — former colleagues, classmates, and past contacts.
  • Show one clear example of what you can do, not a long list of everything.
  • Ask for a short first project with the scope, price, and date in writing.
  • Keep a simple list of every enquiry and follow up once, in writing.
  • One good profile and one good example beat many thin listings.

Start with people who already know your work

Your first clients are more likely to come from people who have seen your work than from strangers. Make a short list of former colleagues, managers, classmates, and anyone who has seen what you can do, and tell each of them, in plain words, what kind of freelance work you are looking for now. Be specific: the service you offer, the kind of client it helps, and the result they get. A vague message that you are "available for freelance work" is easy to ignore; a clear message about one service for one kind of client is easy to pass on. Ask them to keep you in mind and to introduce you if they hear of anyone who needs it — a warm introduction is worth more than a cold message.

Show one clear example, not a long list

A client hiring for the first time wants to see what the finished work looks like. Show one clear example: the problem, what you delivered, and what it let the client do. Keep it short enough to read in a minute, and link to it wherever your name appears — your profile, your message, your proposal. If you have no client work yet, make one sample project for a realistic brief and label it clearly as a sample, so nobody mistakes it for paid client work. One strong example tells a client far more than a long list of tools and courses. Ask for a short first project

A new client is more likely to say yes to a small job than to a large one. Offer a short first project — one deliverable, one deadline, one payment — with the scope, price, and date written down before you start. That small job is a trial for both sides: the client sees how you work, and you see how the client communicates and pays. Write down what is included and what is not, and how many revision rounds are included, so the small job cannot grow quietly into a large one. Where to look next

Once the people you know have heard from you, look further afield. Freelance marketplaces and job boards list briefs from clients who are already looking — apply only to briefs that match the service you offer, and write each application about that brief, not from a generic template. Small local businesses are another route: many need a website update, a set of social posts, or a simple spreadsheet, and a short message offering one specific service can start the conversation. Community groups and professional networks help too, if you answer questions helpfully first and only mention your service when it directly fits. Keep your profile on each platform short and consistent with your example, so a client who finds you anywhere sees the same offer. Keep track of every enquiry

Finding clients is a numbers game, and a simple list keeps it honest. For every enquiry, write down who it was, what they asked for, what you sent, and the date you should follow up. Follow up once, briefly, restating the next step and its date — many first jobs come from the follow-up, not the first message. If there is still no reply, move on rather than discounting your price to chase the work. FAQ

How do I find clients with no experience? Start with people who have seen your work, and show one clear sample project, labelled as a sample. A short first project with a clear scope is easier for a client to say yes to than a long pitch about your potential.

Should I work for free to get my first client? A short paid trial is better than free work. If you do a free sample, keep it small, keep it your own, and agree in writing what happens after it. Free work with no limits rarely turns into paid work.

What should I say in a first message to a client? The problem you can solve for them, one example of similar work, the scope and price of a short first project, and one clear next step. Keep it short enough to read in a minute.

What if a client does not reply? Follow up once, briefly, restating the next step and its date. If there is still no reply, move on. Chasing one silent client costs you the time to find the next one.
