Rereading notes feels productive, but it mostly shows you words you already recognise. To revise without rereading notes, cover your notes and test yourself from memory, write down what you can recall, check only the gaps, and repeat the same material again a few days later — so revision becomes practice at remembering, not another pass over the page.

Key takeaways:

  • Test yourself from memory before you look at your notes again.
  • Write down what you recall, then check only the parts you missed.
  • Space your review across days instead of cramming it into one session.
  • Keep each recall session short and focused on one topic at a time.
  • Use your notes as the answer key, not as the revision itself.

Test yourself before you reread

Start each revision session with a blank page. Write the topic at the top, then write everything you can remember about it without looking at your notes. When you stop, open your notes and fill in only what you missed, in a different colour or with a mark beside it. Those marked gaps are what the next session should target. This works best when your notes already have questions or cues beside each idea — the method in How to Take Notes That Help You Remember Exams 2026 sets that up.

Space your review across days

One long revision session the night before an exam fades fast. Instead, test yourself on a topic, leave it for two or three days, then test yourself on it again. Each time, you will recall more and need your notes less. Fit these short recall sessions into a weekly plan — see How to Make a Study Schedule That Works 2026 on the same blog — and keep a buffer block for topics that still have gaps.

Use short, focused sessions

A recall session does not need to be long. Twenty to thirty minutes on one topic, with a clear question to answer, beats an hour of unfocused rereading. End each session by writing one sentence about what you still cannot recall, so the next session has a starting point. If a topic keeps coming back blank, go back to the original material — a lecture recording, textbook chapter, or course page — and rebuild that part of your notes rather than rereading the same summary again.

Compare revision methods before you choose one

Method Best for What to avoid
Recall from memory Checking what you actually know Peeking at notes halfway through
Spaced review Keeping material over weeks Leaving every topic until the last week
Short focused sessions Fitting revision into a busy week Sessions so short you never test properly
Rereading notes only A first look at new material Mistaking recognition for remembering
Practice questions Exam-style topics Only doing questions you already get right

Turn gaps into your next session

After each recall attempt, your gaps are the plan. List them, in your own words, at the end of the page, and make them the questions for the next session. Over a week, the list should shrink. If it does not, the problem is usually the notes, not your memory — rewrite that section as questions and answers instead of paragraphs. FAQ

Why does rereading feel effective if it is not?

Because the words look familiar. Recognition is not the same as recall — an exam asks you to produce answers without the page in front of you, so practice should match that.

How long should a recall session be?

Short enough to stay focused, usually around twenty to thirty minutes on one topic, with a break before the next topic.

What if I remember almost nothing when I test myself?

That is useful information, not failure. Note the gaps, relearn that section from the original material, and test yourself again in a day or two.

How often should I repeat a topic?

Test it, leave it for a few days, test it again, and keep repeating with longer gaps as your recall improves. Topics with more gaps get repeated sooner.

Can I still use my notes at all?

Yes — as the answer key. Check what you missed, correct it, and close the notes again. The notes support recall; they should not replace it.
