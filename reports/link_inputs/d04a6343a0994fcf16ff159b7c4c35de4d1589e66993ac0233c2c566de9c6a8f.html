Short answer: For most Uber and Lyft drivers, the best starting point is a no- or low-annual-fee card that rewards gas, fuel or EV charging and everyday spending. For rideshare driving, prioritise a card that rewards gas spending, gives you clear, easy-to-read statements, and lets you start a dispute quickly in the app. Check the card terms for any caps, rotating categories, and fees before you apply, and keep business and personal spending separate.

How to compare gas rewards

Gas is often a rideshare driver's largest regular cost, so compare gas rewards on the terms, not on a headline claim. Check whether the reward is capped, for example by a monthly or quarterly spending limit, whether it is a rotating category that changes during the year, or whether it is a flat reward on all spending. A rotating category only helps if you activate it and if gas is included in that period. Read the card terms to see what counts as gas spending, whether fuel at certain stations or convenience-store purchases are treated differently, and whether any reward expires. Do not assume a rate that is not stated in the terms.

Statements and tracking

Clear statements make tax time and budgeting much easier. Look for monthly statements that break spending into categories, such as fuel, tolls, and vehicle costs, and check whether you can download or export transactions for your records. Keeping business and personal spending separate, ideally on a separate card or account, helps you track deductible business costs and avoid mixing personal purchases into your driving records. Review your statement each month for charges you do not recognise, and keep trip receipts alongside it.

If a charge is disputed

If a charge looks wrong, act promptly and keep records. First, check the trip receipt and your own records to confirm what was charged. If the issue is with a merchant, contact the merchant first when that is appropriate. If it is not resolved, file the dispute through your card issuer, usually in the issuer's app or online account, and describe what happened, the date, and the amount. Keep copies of receipts, messages, and any reference number the issuer gives you, and check the issuer's terms for how disputes are handled and how long they may take.

What counts as gas spending? It depends on the card terms. Some issuers count only purchases at gas stations, while others treat fuel bought elsewhere differently. If gas rewards matter to you, check the terms for how gas purchases are defined and whether any purchases are excluded.

How long do disputes usually take? Timelines vary by issuer and by the type of dispute, so check the issuer's terms and any updates the issuer sends about your case. Keep your reference number and records until the dispute is resolved.

How to combine cards for maximum impact

Many drivers benefit from a “primary + secondary” card strategy:

  • Primary card: A flat-rate 2%–3% card for most purchases (simplicity).
  • Secondary card: A gas-focused or dining-focused card used only for those categories to capture higher rates.

Example: If you spend $1,200/month as a driver—$600 on gas, $300 on food, $300 on miscellaneous—using a 3% gas card and a 2% flat card can yield more than a single flat-rate card alone.

Simple math example

  • Gas: $600 at 3% = $18/month
  • Other: $600 at 2% = $12/month
  • Total = $30/month = $360/year

Versus a 2% flat card on $1,200 = $24/month = $288/year. Splitting cards can add $72/yr in this example — and more if you capture higher bonus rates or sign-up bonuses.

Practical tips specific to rideshare & delivery drivers

  • Track mileage AND expenses: Claiming the IRS standard mileage rate or actual expenses both require documentation. Business card statements make the “actual expenses” path easier to substantiate repairs, fuel, and supplies.
  • Look for cell phone protection: Some cards offer cell phone insurance when you pay your monthly phone bill with the card — helpful when you rely on a smartphone for navigation and apps.
  • Utilize statement credits and targeted offers: Cards sometimes send targeted promotions for gas or merchant statement credits — opt in to emails or card apps to catch them.
  • Max out sign-up bonuses cautiously: A sign-up bonus can be lucrative, but only if the spending requirement aligns with your cash flow. Don’t overspend just to meet a bonus if it forces undesirable debt.
  • Consider business cards for employees: If you hire drivers or share vehicles, a business card allows authorized user cards for team spending tracking.

Common mistakes and misconceptions to avoid

  • Chasing the highest headline rate: A 5% gas reward sounds great, but if it’s limited to rotating categories or a specific merchant you don’t use, the net gain may be small.
  • Neglecting fees and interest: Earning 3% back doesn’t help if you carry a high-interest balance. Pay in full each month when possible.
  • Mixing personal and business without separation: It complicates taxes and increases audit risk. Use a dedicated business card or keep meticulous records if you use a personal card.
  • Forgetting to change default payment for subscriptions: If your card changes, update recurring subscriptions and phone auto-pay to maintain protections like cell phone insurance.

FAQ: Quick answers

Should I get a business credit card for my rideshare income?

If you’re 1099, driving full-time, or want clearer expense tracking, a business credit card is usually helpful. It separates expenses, often provides higher limits and offers useful expense reporting for taxes.

Do credit cards help with tax deductions?

Cards don’t change what’s deductible, but they make it easier to document actual vehicle expenses, supplies, phone bills and other business costs—useful at tax time.

Can I use the same card for personal and driving expenses?

Yes, but it’s less ideal. Mixing increases bookkeeping work and raises the chance of missing deductible business expenses. Consider at least using separate cards or tags within your accounting software.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best credit card for rideshare drivers?

One with strong gas/transit cash back, no or low annual fee, and rewards on everyday spend. High-mileage drivers benefit most from fuel and EV-charging bonuses.

Can rideshare drivers deduct expenses?

Yes. Self-employed drivers can deduct either the IRS standard mileage rate or actual vehicle expenses on Schedule C — keep clean records of miles and costs.

Do rideshare drivers need a business credit card?

It isn't required, but using a separate card for driving keeps business and personal spending apart, which simplifies deductions and bookkeeping.

What credit score do you need for a rewards card?

Most cash-back rewards cards want a score around 670 or higher. If you're below that, a secured card builds credit toward qualifying later.

Is a gas card or a cash-back card better for drivers?

A general cash-back card is more flexible, while a gas-branded card can pay more at the pump but only there. Many drivers pair a cash-back card with fuel rewards.
