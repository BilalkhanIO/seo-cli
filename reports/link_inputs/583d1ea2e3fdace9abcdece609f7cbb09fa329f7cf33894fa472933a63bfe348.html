Good notes are notes you can actually use later. Taking notes that help you remember exams means capturing the main idea in your own words during class or reading, then testing yourself from those notes afterwards — not copying everything down and only rereading it the night before.

Key takeaways:

  • Write the main idea in your own words, not a full transcript.
  • Turn each section into a question you can answer without looking.
  • Review briefly soon after class, then again before the exam.
  • Keep one format for lectures and one for reading, so review stays simple.

Start with a clear page layout

Before a lecture or reading session, write the topic, date, and source at the top — for example, the lecture title or the chapter. Leave space for questions on one side and a short summary at the bottom. That layout makes it easy to find a topic later, and it forces you to decide what matters while you are still listening or reading.

Capture ideas, not sentences

During a lecture, write short phrases, key terms, and examples, and mark anything you did not understand with a question mark. If you try to write every sentence, you stop listening for meaning. For reading, pause at the end of each section and write one or two sentences in your own words before moving on. If you cannot explain it briefly, that section needs another look.

Turn notes into questions the same day

Soon after class, read your notes and write a question or cue word beside each main idea. Then cover the notes and try to answer in your own words. Cornell University's Learning Strategies Center describes this pattern as record, question, recite, reflect, and review, and it gives a useful structure for the same habit: record during class, add questions afterwards, recite without looking, and review over time. Review in short rounds, not one long reread

Review your questions the next day, then again a few days later, then before the exam. Each round should be shorter than the last, because you are testing what you remember, not rereading everything. Short sessions only work if you can hold your attention — see How to Stay Focused While Studying 2026. Keep the summary at the bottom of each page short — two or three sentences in your own words is enough to restart your memory.

FAQ

Should I handwrite or type my notes?

Either can work if you test yourself afterwards. Handwriting can slow you down enough to put ideas in your own words, while typing can help you organise long material. Choose the method you will actually review.

How much should I write during a lecture?

Write the main ideas, key terms, and examples, and mark gaps clearly. A complete transcript is hard to review and easy to ignore later.

What if I missed part of a lecture?

Note the gap, check the course material or ask a classmate about that specific point, and fill it in your own words rather than copying someone else's full notes.

How often should I review?

Review briefly soon after class, then again after a few days, then before the exam. Short, repeated testing beats one long reread.

Do I need a different method for every subject?

No. Keep one main method and adjust the format — a table for comparisons, an outline for steps — rather than learning a new system for every class.
