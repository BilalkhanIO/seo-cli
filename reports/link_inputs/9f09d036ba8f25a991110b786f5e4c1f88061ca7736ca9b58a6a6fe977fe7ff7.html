A good university essay starts with the question, not with the first sentence. To write a university essay, read the question until you can say what it is asking in your own words, plan one clear argument that answers it, build each paragraph around a single point with evidence, and edit in passes — for structure first, then for clarity — before you submit.

Key takeaways

  • Restate the question in your own words before you write anything, so you answer what was asked.
  • Plan one clear argument and the points that support it, in order, before drafting.
  • Give each paragraph one point, one piece of evidence, and an explanation of what it proves.
  • Use evidence to support your argument; cut filler that only fills space.
  • Write the introduction and conclusion last, once you know what the essay actually argues.
  • Edit in passes: structure first, then clarity, then references and formatting.

Read the question until you can restate it

Most weak essays do not fail on writing — they fail on answering the wrong question. Read the essay question slowly, more than once, and underline the instruction word: discuss, evaluate, compare, explain, and argue all ask for different things. Then say the question back in your own words, out loud or in one written sentence. If you cannot restate it, you are not ready to plan. Check also what the question does not ask: a question about one author's argument is not an invitation to summarise the whole topic. Keep your restated question visible while you work, and check each paragraph against it as you draft.

Plan one clear argument before you write

An essay needs one argument, not a list of everything you know. Decide what you are claiming — your
answer to the question — and write it as a single sentence. Then list the two or three points that support it, in the order a reader needs them, and note the evidence for each one before you start writing. A short plan like this stops the essay from drifting, and it makes the first draft much faster to write, because each paragraph already has a job. If you take notes while you research, keep them organised by the point they support, not by the source they came from — the method in Best Note-Taking Methods for Students (2026) works well for this.

Build each paragraph around one point

A paragraph should do one thing. Start it with a sentence that states its point, then give the evidence — a quotation, a finding, or an example from a source — and then explain, in your own words, what that evidence shows and how it supports your argument. If a paragraph contains two points, split it. If it contains no evidence, it is probably an opinion dressed as an argument. When you gather evidence from textbooks and articles, read for the point you need rather than reading every page in order — see How to Read a Textbook Faster for Exams 2026.

Use evidence, not filler

Evidence is what separates a university essay from a school essay. Cite the sources you actually used, quote sparingly, and paraphrase the rest in your own words — a long string of quotations reads as if someone else wrote the essay. Every quotation needs a sentence after it that explains what it proves; a quotation on its own proves nothing. Cut sentences that repeat the same point in different words, and cut background that the question never asked for. If you use an AI tool to brainstorm angles, prompt it with the exact question and ask for counter-arguments, not a finished essay — the prompting method is in How to Write Effective AI Prompts (2026) — and always check your university's rules on AI use before you use one. Purdue University's Online Writing Lab has a clear guide to essay writing, from planning to drafting: https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/essay_writing/index.html

Write the introduction and conclusion last

Write the body first, then the introduction and conclusion. The introduction should tell the reader what question you are answering and what your argument is, in a few sentences — it does not need a dramatic opening line. The conclusion should answer the question directly, restate your argument in light of the evidence, and stop: it is not the place for new evidence or a new point. If your conclusion introduces something new, that material belongs in the body, or it does not belong at all. The University of North Carolina Writing Center explains how an argument is built and tested, which is a useful check for both sections: https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/argument/

Edit in passes, not in one read

Do not try to fix everything in a single read-through. First pass: structure — does each paragraph answer the question, in order, with one point each? Second pass: clarity — cut long sentences, vague words, and repeated points; read the essay out loud if a sentence feels tangled. Third pass: references and formatting — every source cited, every quotation attributed, the reference list complete. Leave a gap between drafting and editing if you can: even a few hours away from the text makes errors easier to spot. For the revision step after drafting, the method in How to Revise Without Rereading Notes 2026 works for essays too — check your argument against your notes, not the other way round.

FAQ

How long should a university essay be?

Follow the word count your course set. If no count was given, ask your tutor — do not guess a length from another course's essay, because expectations differ.

Can I use "I" in an essay?

It depends on your subject and your tutor's guidance. Some subjects accept a first-person argument; others expect a neutral voice throughout. Check the guidance for your course before you decide.

How many sources do I need?

Enough to support each point you make — there is no fixed number that fits every essay. One strong source per point beats five weak ones, and every source you cite should actually be used in the argument.

Can I use AI to write my essay?

Check your university's rules first — they differ, and submitting AI-written work as your own can breach academic rules. Using a tool to brainstorm or to check your own draft is a different thing from having it write the essay, and your university's policy is the one that counts.

What if I run out of time?

Write the body paragraphs first, in plan order, with the evidence you have. A complete argument with a short introduction and conclusion is worth more than a polished opening with no ending.
