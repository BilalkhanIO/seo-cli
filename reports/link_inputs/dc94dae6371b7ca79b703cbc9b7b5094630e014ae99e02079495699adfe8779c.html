What Counts as a "Good" Savings Rate in Europe in 2026?

Savings rates in the eurozone track the European Central Bank's deposit facility rate. When the ECB cuts rates, banks follow within weeks — so any specific number you see in an article (including this one) has a shelf life. The reliable way to judge an offer is relative, not absolute:

  • At or above the ECB deposit rate: excellent — usually a promotional or fintech offer competing hard for deposits.
  • 0.5–1% below the ECB rate: normal for established online banks.
  • Near zero: typical of large traditional branch banks. If your money sits here, you're leaving returns on the table.

You can check the current ECB deposit facility rate on the official ECB key interest rates page before comparing any offer.

Where Europeans Actually Find the Best Rates

Three categories dominate the high-interest savings market in Europe. Always verify the live rate on the provider's own site — rates below change frequently.

1. Savings marketplaces (Raisin)

Platforms like Raisin (WeltSparen in Germany) let you open one account and spread deposits across dozens of partner banks in different EU countries, each covered by its national deposit guarantee scheme. This is often where the highest fixed-term rates in Europe appear, because smaller banks from Italy, Latvia, or Portugal compete for cross-border deposits.

2. Fintechs and neobanks

Trade Republic, N26, Revolut, Bunq, and Scalable Capital have all used interest on uninvested cash as a customer-acquisition tool. These accounts are convenient and often pay close to the ECB rate — but read the fine print: some pay interest only up to a cap (e.g., the first €50,000), some require a paid subscription tier, and some hold your cash in money market funds rather than bank deposits, which changes your protection.

3. Direct online banks

Country-specific online banks — ING, DKB, Consorsbank (Germany), Fortuneo and BoursoBank (France), Openbank (Spain) — regularly run welcome rates for new customers. These are genuine but temporary: typically 3–6 months at a boosted rate, then a drop to a base rate. They're worth using if you don't mind moving money once a year.

The €100,000 Rule: How EU Deposit Protection Works

This is the part most comparison articles skip, and it matters more than 0.2% of extra interest. Under the EU's Deposit Guarantee Schemes Directive, deposits up to €100,000 per depositor, per bank are protected if the bank fails. Key details:

  • The guarantee comes from the country where the bank is licensed, not where you live. A Lithuanian-licensed fintech is covered by Lithuania's scheme.
  • The limit applies per bank, so €300,000 split across three separately licensed banks is fully protected; €300,000 at one bank is not.
  • Joint accounts are protected up to €100,000 per account holder.
  • Money held as e-money or in money market funds (common with some fintech "interest" products) is safeguarded differently and may not carry the same €100,000 deposit guarantee. Always check whether your balance is a true bank deposit.

The European Banking Authority maintains an overview of national schemes on its depositor protection page.

Instant Access vs Fixed-Term: Which Should You Pick?

Most savers should hold both, in this order:

  1. Emergency fund (3–6 months of expenses) → instant-access account. Accept a slightly lower rate for the ability to withdraw same-day.
  2. Money you won't need for 1–3 years → fixed-term deposit. In 2026's falling-rate environment, locking a rate can beat a variable account that gets cut every quarter.
  3. Money you won't need for 5+ years → savings accounts are the wrong tool; historically, diversified investing has outpaced deposit rates. That's a separate decision beyond this article.

A 5-Minute Checklist Before Opening Any Account

  • Which country licenses the bank? Confirm the deposit guarantee scheme behind it.
  • Is the rate promotional? Note the base rate you'll earn after the welcome period ends.
  • Is there a balance cap on interest? Some accounts pay the headline rate only on part of your balance.
  • Deposit or fund? Confirm your money is a bank deposit, not a money market fund, if the €100,000 guarantee matters to you.
  • Withholding tax: interest earned abroad is usually taxable in your country of residence; some countries deduct tax at source, and you may need to declare foreign interest on your annual return.
  • Currency: if you live outside the eurozone (Poland, Czechia, Sweden…), a euro account adds exchange-rate risk to your savings.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I open a savings account in another EU country?

Yes. EU rules give residents the right to open a basic payment account anywhere in the EU, and savings marketplaces like Raisin make cross-border saving straightforward — you complete one identity verification and can then place deposits with partner banks across Europe.

Are fintech savings accounts as safe as bank accounts?

It depends on how they hold your money. If the fintech has a banking licence (or parks your cash with a licensed partner bank as a true deposit), the €100,000 guarantee applies. If your balance sits in a money market fund, it isn't a guaranteed deposit — it's a low-risk investment. Neither is "bad," but they are not the same thing.

Do I pay tax on savings interest in Europe?

In almost every European country, yes — interest is taxable income. Rules, allowances, and withholding rates vary widely by country (Germany's Sparer-Pauschbetrag allowance works very differently from French flat tax, for example), so check your national rules or ask a local tax adviser before assuming anything.

Should I lock money into a fixed-term deposit if rates are falling?

Generally, falling-rate environments favour fixed terms: you secure today's rate while variable accounts drift down. The trade-off is liquidity — most fixed deposits penalise or forbid early withdrawal, so only lock money you're confident you won't need.

Frequently asked questions

Are savings accounts in Europe safe?

Deposits at EU-authorised banks are protected up to €100,000 per person, per bank, under the national Deposit Guarantee Scheme. Confirm the bank is covered before depositing.

What is the best type of savings account?

It depends on access needs. Instant-access suits an emergency fund; fixed-term deposits pay more if you can lock the money away.

Can I open a savings account in another EU country?

Often yes — pan-European deposit marketplaces let residents of one EU country open accounts at banks in another, while keeping DGS protection.

Is savings interest taxed in Europe?

Usually yes, in your country of residence, though rates and allowances vary widely. Some countries offer tax-advantaged savings — check local rules.

What does AER mean?

Annual Equivalent Rate shows the interest you'd earn over a year including compounding, so you can compare accounts on a like-for-like basis.
