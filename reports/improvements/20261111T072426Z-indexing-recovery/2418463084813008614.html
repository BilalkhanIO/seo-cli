A freelance contract checklist helps you agree the work before you start it. At its simplest, a contract should state what you will deliver, when you will deliver it, how and when you will be paid, how many revisions are included, and who owns the work after payment — in plain language both sides understand. This is general information, not legal advice, and local rules differ, so get local advice for high-value or complex work.

Key takeaways:

  • Agree scope, deliverables, and what counts as done before work starts.
  • State payment timing, method, and what happens if payment is late.
  • Limit revisions and price extra changes separately.
  • State who owns the work, and when ownership transfers.
  • Write down how either side can end the work, and what is paid for work done so far.

Start with scope and deliverables

Write exactly what you will deliver, in what format, and by what date. List what is not included as well, so a small request does not quietly become a second project. Add a simple definition of done — for example, a delivered file in an agreed format, or a page published after client approval. If the client must supply text, images, or access, write that down with a deadline too.

Agree payment terms in writing

State the fee basis you have agreed — for example, a fixed fee for the project or a fee for a defined block of work — plus when invoices are sent and when payment is due. State what happens if payment is late, and whether work pauses until payment arrives. Keep this section free of invented rates: write only the terms you and the client actually agreed. Set revisions and change requests

State how many revision rounds are included, how long the client has to request them, and how extra changes are priced. Without this, revisions can continue indefinitely and delay payment. A short change rule — new scope means a new written agreement and a new price — protects both sides and keeps the original deadline realistic.

Cover ownership and use

State who owns the finished work and when ownership transfers. A common approach is that ownership transfers after full payment, while your general tools, templates, and earlier work remain yours. If the client needs to use drafts or parts of the work before payment, write down exactly what they may use. Add ending, confidentiality, and client duties

State how either side can end the agreement, what notice is needed, and what is paid for work already done. Add a short confidentiality rule for client material you see during the work, and list the client's duties — supplying material on time, giving feedback by an agreed date, and naming one person who can approve the work.

FAQ

Do I need a contract for a small job?

Yes, even a short written agreement helps. A one-page agreement with scope, fee, payment timing, and ownership is far clearer than messages alone.

What if the client sends their own contract?

Read it against this checklist, note anything missing or unclear, and ask for changes in writing before you sign. Do not sign a contract you have not read.

Should ownership transfer before payment?

Many freelancers transfer ownership after full payment. Whatever you agree, write it down clearly so there is no dispute about use before payment.

How do I handle a request outside the scope?

Pause, describe the extra work in writing, agree a price and deadline for it, and only then start it. That keeps the original project and payment clear.

Is this legal advice?

No. This checklist is general information for US, UK, Canada, and EU readers. Local contract and tax rules differ, so get local professional advice for complex or high-value work.
