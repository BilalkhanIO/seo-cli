A freelance proposal wins work when the client can see, in the first minute, that you understood their problem and know what result you will deliver. To write a freelance proposal that wins work, open with the client's problem in their own terms, describe the result and deliverables in plain language, state the scope, price, and timeline clearly, and end with one clear next step. This is general information, not legal or financial advice, and local contract and tax rules differ.

Key takeaways:

  • Open with the client's problem, not with your own biography.
  • Describe the result and deliverables, not just the tasks you will do.
  • State exactly what is included, so extra work is priced separately.
  • Give a clear price basis, timeline, and one next step.
  • Keep the proposal short enough to read in a few minutes.

Start with the client's problem

Read the brief twice before writing anything. Then open the proposal by restating the problem the client described, in their own terms, and naming the result they want. A client who sees their own problem described accurately will keep reading; a proposal that opens with a long list of the freelancer's skills gives them no reason to. If anything in the brief is unclear, ask a short, specific question before you quote — guessing at the scope is how proposals fail after they are accepted.

Describe the result, not just the tasks

Instead of listing only what you will do, describe what the client will have when the work is finished: the deliverable, its format, and what it will let them do. For example, a finished page they can publish, a report they can act on, or a set of files in an agreed format. Then list the main steps briefly, so the client can see the plan without wading through detail. Keep the scope tight: what is included, what is not included, and how many revision rounds are included. The contract wording that supports this is covered in the Freelance Contract Checklist.

State scope, price, and timeline clearly

Give the price basis you are using — a fixed fee for the project or a fee for a defined block of work — plus when invoices are sent and when payment is due. Do not publish or promise a specific rate that fits every freelancer; price the defined scope in front of you, and check it against your floor rate before you send it. The method for working out that floor rate is in How to Price Freelance Work Without Undercharging on the same blog. Give a realistic timeline with a start point and a delivery date, and say what you need from the client, and by when, to meet it.

End with one clear next step

Close with a single action: a short call on a stated day, a reply confirming the scope, or a signed agreement by a date. One next step is easier to act on than a list of options, and it shows the client you will manage the work, not just do it. FAQ

How long should a freelance proposal be?

Short enough to read in a few minutes. Cover the problem, the result, the scope, the price basis, the timeline, and the next step — and cut anything that does not help the client decide.

Should I include my full work history?

No. Include only experience directly relevant to this brief, in a sentence or two, and link to examples if the client wants more.

What if the brief is vague?

Ask short, specific questions before you quote. A proposal written on a guessed scope usually fails when the real scope appears.

Should I offer different price options?

Only if the options genuinely differ in scope or result — for example, a basic deliverable versus a fuller one. Options that differ only in price confuse the decision.

What if the client does not reply?

Follow up once, briefly, restating the next step and its date. If there is still no reply, move on rather than discounting the price to chase the work.

Is this legal advice?

No. This is general information for US, UK, Canada, and EU readers. Local contract and tax rules differ, so get local professional advice for complex or high-value work.
